## Overview

Part 1 of a two-part essay on where 3D printing fits in the mechanical-parts economy. David lines up six plastic caps — an injection-molded prototype ($5,000 in tooling), an Old Spice gel cap, a Wilson tennis-ball lid, a Folgers coffee lid, a Mason jar lid, and a 3D-printed cap — and asks which is best engineered. The answer surprises: the cheapest commodity caps are the most refined and best-tolerated, because they've been competed-on for decades. This video states the problem; [[videos/economics-of-mechanical-designs-part-2|Part 2]] gives the solution.

## Key takeaways

- A prototype represents the *fewest* engineering hours, not the most — and a prototype injection-molded part still requires ~$5,000 of tooling.
- The companies behind Old Spice and Folgers are selling the *product*, not the cap — so the cap absorbs less engineering attention than the company's hero feature.
- Commodity caps (PVC pipe caps, Mason jar lids) have the most refined tolerances *and* the lowest unit cost — many manufacturers competing for decades drove both up and down at once.
- A 3D-printed CPVC cap costs ~$0.20 in plastic but ~$6 to actually produce next-day; the off-the-shelf commodity ($1.50) wins on every axis.
- Setting up the categories: **prototype** vs. **product** vs. **commodity** — three different economic regimes, each with a different cost driver.
