## Overview

[[entities/people/david-malawey|David]] makes the case that rags are a foundational lab technology that [[entities/brands/texas-am|Texas A&M ETID]] has lacked since 2016, and that the absence cascades into poor student outcomes and weak job-interview confidence. He walks through rag selection (cotton vs. microfiber), commercial laundry-rotation services like Cintas/Grainger-supplied programs, why paper towels don't substitute, and how rags integrate with solvents (acetone, isopropyl alcohol, mineral spirits) for vapor-smoothing and cleanup. He also released a PDF memo to university administrators on GitHub.

## Key takeaways

- Rags are infrastructure — without them, students can't responsibly use solvents, finish parts, or clean tools, and professional habits never form.
- Cotton white rags are the workhorse: absorbent, lint-low, washable, durable. Microfiber serves precise/optical cleaning. Paper towels are wrong for the job — they shed lint, can't handle solvents, and abuse the bathroom dispenser when used as a substitute.
- Commercial rag services (Cintas, Grainger-style suppliers) provide weekly pickup/delivery cycles for a flat fee — the same model fraternities and restaurants use.
- A rag should stay with a project for its duration: it accumulates the project's debris and absorbs the project's spills, then goes to laundry.
- Acetone for vapor-smoothing prints; [[entities/materials/isopropyl-alcohol|isopropyl alcohol]] is gentler on skin and less aggressive; mineral spirits for heavier cleanup. Rag matched to solvent.
- The cascade: no rags → students avoid solvents → no surface finishing → poor parts → low confidence in interviews → "second-class engineers."
- David published a PDF memo on GitHub for administrators to formally request improvements.

## Techniques demonstrated

- [[concepts/rag-as-infrastructure|Rag as infrastructure]] — treating rags as a managed lab consumable.

- Vapor smoothing of 3D prints with acetone-soaked rags.
