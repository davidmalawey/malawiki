## Overview

David's first attempt to show that thermodynamics — the universe's drive toward energy balance — is the hidden axis behind almost every design decision. He uses a bowed tabletop, a flaking lab floor, a colander, and a jack stand to argue that designs either cooperate with thermodynamics (and degrade gracefully) or fight it (and fail catastrophically when they fail).

## Key takeaways

- Every nonporous surface humans build is a fight with thermodynamics; air, moisture, heat, and pressure all want to equalize.
- A 1 mm length change in a 1000 mm board is only 0.1% — easy to achieve via humidity — and that's enough to bow a tabletop visibly.
- The same paint formulation that survived two years on outdoor plywood failed on the lab's concrete floor because the floor's moisture-vapor problem made it the wrong job for that paint.
- Grout is intentionally porous to let moisture migrate; sealing it "ramps up the battle with thermo" instead of solving the cleaning problem.
- Cooperative-with-thermo designs (a colander) just perform "less good" when damaged. Fighting-with-thermo designs (a jack stand) stop working entirely when damaged.
- Understanding thermo lets you hack devices you've never been trained on — David can repair a pressure lantern despite having no lantern training, just from valve and thermo fundamentals.
- Call to action: pick one recurring scenario in your work, study its thermodynamics, verify with GPT, and extrapolate.

## Techniques demonstrated

- Reading an outcome (peeling paint, a leaking tire) as a "lost battle with thermodynamics."
- Distinguishing thermodynamically cooperative vs. thermodynamically fighting designs as a categorization for evaluating any artifact.
