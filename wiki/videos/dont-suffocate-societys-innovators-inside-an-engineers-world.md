## Overview

[[entities/people/david-malawey|David]] argues that a modern-day Ben Franklin or Thomas Edison would struggle to survive in today's U.S. economy, and tells a story from his Toyota engine-development days to illustrate why. While working a three-month project on the [[concepts/flywheel-bolt|flywheel bolt]] of the four-cylinder ZR engine — the Camry/Corolla family — he used a small internal Toyota application called Neitomokun ("screw friend") to model how bolt coating affects clamping torque. The story sets up a broader claim: society's "engineer-entrepreneur" archetype gets stuck on local maxima because the surrounding system doesn't reward the depth of work that producing a real invention requires.

## Key takeaways

- The "innovator" archetype David has in mind is closer to Ben Franklin / Thomas Edison than to Bezos / Musk — a hands-on inventor, not a venture-scale operator.
- Toyota's depth: a single engineer can spend three months on five bolts because changing the coating shifts the friction coefficient, which shifts the achieved clamp load at a given torque, which propagates into the engine's operational range.
- The same flywheel bolt is reused after dealer servicing — so the design has to tolerate friction from a previously-coated, partially-cleaned bolt, not just a fresh one.
- Toyota's pneumatic torque guns on the assembly line embody all that engineering work as a single specification number.
- Outside Toyota, individual innovators rarely have the resources, time, or institutional backing to do that depth of work — they get stuck on [[concepts/local-maxima|local maxima]] instead of pushing through to a real invention.

## Concepts

- [[concepts/flywheel-bolt|Flywheel bolt engineering]] — friction, coating, reuse, torque-to-clamp mapping.
- [[concepts/local-maxima|Local maxima]] in design — getting stuck at "good enough" because the path to genuinely better is too expensive for one person.
