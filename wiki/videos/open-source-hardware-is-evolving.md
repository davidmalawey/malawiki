## Overview

A 30-minute essay arguing that open source hardware is happening "by accident" as mainstream electrified products fragment into modules. [[entities/people/david-malawey|David]] traces the analogy from the NASA-room-sized computer through the modular PC and CD-ROM era, applies it to mechatronics (battery + motor + controller decoupling in tools like Echo's modular trimmer), and finishes with a critique of mechatronics undergrad programs that try to teach five disciplines at once.

## Key takeaways

- Outdoor power tools (Echo, weed whackers) are decoupling into battery + motor + control + interchangeable end-effector. That decoupling is open-sourcing in disguise — the moment a connection becomes a published standard, the system is "open" at that node.
- Robotics is following the PC's path: NASA-room → desktop → consumer device required modularity (CD-ROM, power supplies, monitors) defined by standards.
- "Open source software" works because every piece is fully digital, fully parametric, copy-pasteable. Hardware needs the same — characterized modules, not exposed CAD internals.
- A robotics company should stop reinventing chainsaw mechanisms and instead buy the chainsaw module, then design only the integration. [[entities/brands/boston-dynamics|Boston Dynamics]]-style monolithic engineering is the wrong model.
- Mechatronics degrees are stuck because the field equals five disciplines. The fix is technician-level proficiency in each, mastery in one — the integrator role.
- Once standards exist (volts, RPM, mounting interface) you only need a few parameters to digitize a physical module — characterization, not internal CAD.
