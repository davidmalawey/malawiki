## Overview

[[entities/people/david-malawey|David]]'s unscripted lab tour around a single organizing principle: every tool, drawer, and container should be accessible with one hand — and ideally visible without any hand. This doubles output simply because your other hand stays free for the work. He layers in two more themes: products today have lost the *mass* that previous generations took for granted (so weighted bottoms, metal switches, and ballasted doors quietly enhance every workflow), and standardizing on shared profiles (e.g., 2020 aluminum extrusion, camera tripod threads) eliminates one-off CAD work.

## Key takeaways

- One-handed access is the unifying metric: see-through containers, single-layer drawers, and turning spool axes so they don't roll when you close the drawer.
- Cheap "Helping Hands" tools flex under load and are useless for soldering — switch to camera-grade ball-mount arms with rigid threaded fittings.
- Multiply safety equipment per workstation rather than centralizing — convenience is what determines compliance.
- Multiply trash cans for the same reason; a few extra bags is trivial compared to the behavioral lift.
- A shop vac with a sense-and-trigger outlet (the saw turns it on automatically, vacuum runs 5–10 s afterward) is "all hardware, no software" and never fails on a confused team member.
- Adding [[concepts/mass|mass]] to objects (weighted bin bottoms, metal foot switches, ballasted cabinet doors) restores the implicit physical stability that modern lightweight products discarded.
- Standardize on a shared mounting language — 2020 aluminum extrusion, camera cold-shoe threads — so a single bracket published once unlocks thousands of tool-free attachments.
- Toyota assembly-line training: before David was allowed on the [[entities/brands/toyota|Toyota]] line installing 500 oil pans/day, he had to pass a two-handed-screw drill — that skill compounds.

## Techniques demonstrated

- [[concepts/one-handed-access|One-handed access]] design.
- Per-station duplication of safety glasses, hearing protection, trash receptacles.
- Magnet bars on drawer fronts as high-frequency tool storage.
- Mass-loading cheap doors and bins so they behave like expensive ones.
- Hardware-triggered tool/vacuum interlocks.
- Standardizing on 2020 extrusion + camera threads instead of designing custom 3D-printed brackets.
