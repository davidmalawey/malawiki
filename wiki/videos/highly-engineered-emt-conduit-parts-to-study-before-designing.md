## Overview

[[entities/people/david-malawey|David]] walks through a bin of EMT-conduit-aisle hardware (handy boxes, chase nipples, closure plugs, compression connectors, threaded couplings, knockout seals, angle connectors, DIN-rail bits, switch boxes, hanging brackets) and argues that mechanical designers should study these mass-produced, low-cost parts before drawing 3D-printed equivalents. The thesis: the [[entities/projects/openbox-project|OpenBox]] handy box alone replaces thousands of one-off printed enclosures, and the surrounding ecosystem of conduit fittings gives a designer a ready-made vocabulary of solved problems.

## Key takeaways

- The conduit/electrical aisle of a big-box hardware store is one of the densest concentrations of well-engineered, high-volume, low-cost mechanical parts a designer can buy.
- The [[entities/tools/handy-box|handy box]] (steel stamped electrical box) is the chassis David uses for the [[entities/projects/openbox-project|OpenBox]] ecosystem — it's stronger, cheaper, and more rigid than equivalent printed boxes.
- Each part embeds answers to a specific design question: chase nipples bridge two boxes, closure plugs seal an unused knockout, [[concepts/compression-connector|compression connectors]] resist pull-out and twist on EMT, threaded couplings join rigid conduit, angle connectors route around corners.
- Studying a parts ecosystem first lets you adopt [[concepts/borrowing-tolerances|borrowing tolerances]] from the existing standard rather than reinventing them.
- Trade sizes (e.g. "1/2 inch" EMT) are nominal — don't expect literal dimensions; learn the trade convention.
