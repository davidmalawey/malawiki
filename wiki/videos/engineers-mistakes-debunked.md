## Overview

A meandering reflection that starts as a comment-section debate about what counts as an "engineer's mistake" and ends as the rationale for open-sourcing David's entire mechanical engineering bachelor's degree. He argues that intention is what separates a mistake from an improvement, and that publishing every homework, exam, and red-pen mark from a four-year degree could give a handful of motivated learners — anywhere in the world — the power to understand any [[entities/people/leonardo-da-vinci|da Vinci]] invention.

## Key takeaways

- A thermodynamics exam scored 60 then corrected to 64.6 — both the student and the professor made mistakes; this is normal in engineering education.
- Software version numbers (major.minor.bug-fix) are a reframing of mistakes; "perfect" software would have one revision and never change.
- Intention, not outcome, separates a mistake from an improvement. Engineers can choose to relabel everything as "lessons" after the fact.
- Engineers' mistakes get defined by their boss; entrepreneurs' mistakes get defined by themselves; students' by their professors.
- Once you're on a path no one has walked, comparison becomes dangerous — you start borrowing benchmarks from unrelated work.
- The Open ME Project (qr.net/openmeproject) publishes the entire four-year mechanical engineering bachelor's degree — every homework, every exam, every grade and handwritten note.
- Mechanical engineering knowledge alone, even without other disciplines, is enough to understand every [[entities/people/leonardo-da-vinci|da Vinci]] invention. Adding it to the existing open-source community could "change the world overnight."
