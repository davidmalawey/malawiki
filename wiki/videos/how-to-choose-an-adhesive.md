## Overview

David's first cut at a comprehensive adhesives reference, drawn from 10 years of trial-and-error and 10-spreadsheets-deep notes. The video walks through major adhesive families, the three [[concepts/bonding-types|bonding mechanisms]] (chemical, mechanical, tacky/contact), and a decision framework: pick the bond by intersecting substrate material, application method, and load/environment requirements. Lots of destructive tests with [[entities/tools/torque-wrench|torque wrench]] and peel pulls.

## Key takeaways

- [[entities/materials/silicone|Silicone]] is the closest thing to a "cure-all" adhesive: bonds to almost every substrate, easy to work with, minimal PPE, large containers cost-effective, and it bonds to itself with ~100% strength after curing. Limitation: it cures from the outside in — thick beads stay uncured inside, so it isn't a structural fill adhesive.
- [[concepts/bonding-types|Three bond types David teaches]]:
  1. **Chemical bond** — adhesive (or its solvent) fuses with the substrate; polymer chains merge. Strongest. [[entities/materials/pvc|PVC]] cement, [[entities/materials/abs|ABS]] cement.
  2. **Mechanical bond** — adhesive interlocks with surface texture / pores. Why surface prep (sanding, melting ridges) matters.
  3. **Tacky / contact bond** — chemically resistant substrates leave you with surface-only adhesion. Common with [[entities/materials/nylon|nylon]], [[entities/materials/ptfe-teflon|PTFE]], polyolefins.
- [[entities/materials/hot-melt-glue|Hot melt glue]] ranks just behind silicone for general use — fast, strong on porous substrates, bad on chemically-resistant plastics.
- [[entities/materials/jb-weld|JB Weld]] (steel-reinforced [[entities/materials/epoxy|epoxy]]) is rigid and powerful but brittle and a poor choice when one substrate flexes (e.g., bonded to [[entities/materials/rubber|rubber]]).
- [[entities/materials/construction-adhesive|Construction adhesive]] (water-based or solvent-based) is great for wood and gap-filling; doesn't survive submersion.
- [[entities/materials/pvc-cement|PVC cement]] is a two-part system: primer dissolves the surface, then cement chemically welds the parts. Real solvent-welding, not adhesion.
- [[entities/materials/two-part-epoxy|Two-part epoxies]] vary widely. Mixing ratio matters; under-mixed epoxy stays soft. Fiberglass epoxy embeds fibers and changes failure mode entirely.
- [[entities/materials/contact-cement|Contact cements]] like E6000 hold well in shear but fail in peel. Match the bond mechanism to the load direction.
- Always read the [[concepts/adhesive-datasheet|adhesive datasheet]] (or product page on Mouser/manufacturer site) — it lists compatible substrates, cure time, temperature range, and viscosity. Most adhesive failures are foreseeable from the datasheet.
- [[entities/brands/3m|3M]] and similar major-brand industrial adhesives outperform consumer brands when the application is critical.

## Techniques demonstrated

- [[concepts/adhesive-selection-method|Adhesive selection by material × method × requirement]] — David's working framework.
- Torque-wrench destructive testing of bonded shank-in-sleeve joints (JB Weld held to ~80 in-lb before internal failure).
- Peel testing on rigid-vs-rubber bonded plates.
- [[concepts/surface-preparation|Surface preparation]] via sanding or melting ridges to convert smooth plastics into mechanically-bondable surfaces (ties to the [[videos/hack-a-soldering-iron-with-a-mechanical-engineer|soldering iron hack video]]).
- Tube-preservation trick: cap silicone tubes with a Vaseline + silicone air barrier so they last months.
- Mixing colorants (mica powder) into silicone and epoxy for art / ID purposes.
