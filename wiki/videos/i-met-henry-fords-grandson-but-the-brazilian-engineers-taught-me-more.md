## Overview

A long-form commentary video where David traces his journey from meeting William Clay Ford at Toyota in 2012 (and finding the great-grandson had no technological wisdom to share) through his Brazilian grad-school friendships, three months traveling Brazil, and his current collaboration with Nigerian engineers on the SCUTTLE robotics platform. His thesis: in highly developed economies, technology drifts away from meeting needs and toward serving wealth, IP, and marketing — while in developing economies, every technology project sits inches from a real need. He argues the U.S. has lost true entrepreneurship (which requires real risk tolerance) and that engineering progress now depends on collaborating with Nigeria, Brazil, and Malaysia where opportunity outweighs perceived risk.

## Key takeaways

- William Clay Ford had no book recommendation when asked — when wealth becomes the priority, technological wisdom decays.
- Brazilian engineering friends "transcended" class via technology + education + credentials, but corruption ceilings prevent broader societal impact.
- Risk tolerance correlates inversely with abundance — U.S. abundance has produced "small-business" curricula that aren't entrepreneurship.
- A degree in entrepreneurship is a contradiction: you're seeking a template to eliminate risk from a discipline defined by risk.
- Nigeria is currently developing faster than the U.S. ever did, with internet access from day one — engineering there meets real needs almost automatically.
- A water valve in the U.S. earns "you should patent it"; the same valve in Nigeria earns "we could grow more cattle."
- Society's *association* with wealth (cousin-supports-cousin in Nigeria vs. resentment in the U.S.) is a hidden multiplier on entrepreneurial outcomes.
